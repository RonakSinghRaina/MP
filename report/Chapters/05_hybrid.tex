\chapter{\hybrid}
\label{ch:hybrid}
This chapter gives the results of \hybrid{}, described in
\cref{sec:hybrid_design}; \cref{ch:drivers} then tests which of its components
actually matter.

\section{Results on synthetic data}
On the synthetic data the model performs very well, but as
\cref{sec:gap} showed, this data is much easier than real observations, so
these results are mainly a check that the model trains correctly.
On the 1024$\times$265 dataset the version used in the rest of this report (8
filters, 593{,}842 parameters) reached an \Fone{} of 0.9877.

The width of the network can be reduced a long way at little cost
(\cref{tab:width}). Going from 32 to 8 filters in the first layer removes 94\%
of the parameters and lowers \Fone{} by only 0.006; only at 4 filters does the
model clearly get worse.

\section{Results on LOFAR}
\label{sec:hybrid_lofar}

\subsection{Class weighting}
With class weighting (RFI pixels given 57 times the weight of clean ones), the
model reached a maximum \Fone{} of $0.6467 \pm 0.0079$ over three seeds;
without it, $0.6603 \pm 0.0040$. Removing the weight improved the result for
every seed (paired $t = 6.03$ with 2 degrees of freedom, significant at the 5\%
level), so the unweighted model is used from here on.

Interestingly, the area under the ROC curve moved the opposite way, falling
from 0.980 to 0.938. The ROC curve measures how well the model ranks RFI pixels
above clean ones, while \Fone{} measures the quality of the final yes/no
decision. Class weighting improved the ranking but made the decisions worse,
which shows that a high ROC area alone does not mean a model flags RFI well.

\subsection{Comparison with published methods}
\Cref{tab:final} and \cref{fig:final} compare the final model with the other
methods on the 109 human-labelled test images. \hybrid{} reaches a maximum
\Fone{} of $0.6603 \pm 0.0040$; with the threshold fixed in advance on the
validation set, the pooled \Fone{} is $0.6561 \pm 0.0044$. This is 0.11 above
\tfunet{} and 0.062 above RFI-Net (0.5979), the best result reported by
Mesarcik et al.\ (one-sample $t = 27.2$ with 2 degrees of freedom). It is also
0.040 above Mesarcik et al.'s U-Net when we ran their own code with their
settings (0.6207, \cref{sec:mesarcik_rerun}). It is
achieved while training only on \aoflagger{}'s labels, which themselves score
0.5698 against the human expert.

\section{Fine-tuning with expert labels}
\label{sec:expert_ft}
Every label used to train \hybrid{} comes from \aoflagger{}, which itself
scores only 0.5698 against the human expert, so part of the model's remaining
error may be learned from its labels. Expert labels exist only for the 109
test images. To measure what some of them add without ever testing on an image
whose expert label the model has seen, I used five-fold cross-validation over
these 109 images. Images that nearly duplicate each other (77 groups, whose
expert masks overlap by more than 0.3) were always kept in the same fold. In
each fold, 21--22 images formed the test set, 19 other images the validation
set, and the remaining 68--69 images could be used for fine-tuning. Each of the
three trained models was fine-tuned with its architecture unchanged, using the
same loss computed only on the labelled pixels, a learning rate of $10^{-4}$
and random flips in time and frequency; the checkpoint was chosen on the 19
validation images. In a pilot on 20 images, the same fine-tuning with
\aoflagger{}'s labels instead of the expert's gave no gain, so any improvement
comes from the expert labels and not from the extra training.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption[Fine-tuning with expert labels]{Fine-tuning \hybrid{} on expert labels, scored on all 109 images by
    five-fold cross-validation (three seeds). These results use expert labels
    in training, so they are not comparable with \cref{tab:final}.}
  \label{tab:expert_ft}
  \begin{tabular}{lcc}
    \toprule

    \midrule
    No fine-tuning & $0.6603 \pm 0.0040$ & $0.6534 \pm 0.0035$ \\

Fine-tuning for 600 steps on all available expert labels raised the maximum
\Fone{} over the 109 images from 0.6603 to $0.6747 \pm 0.0083$
(\cref{tab:expert_ft}), an improvement for all three seeds (by 0.012 to
0.020; paired $t = 5.69$ with 2 degrees of freedom, significant at the 5\%
level). The pooled \Fone{}, with each fold's threshold chosen on its
validation images, rises by a similar 0.015. This is level with RFDL's results when it too is trained with expert
labels\footcite{vanzyl2024rfdl}, $0.669 \pm 0.022$ and $0.675 \pm 0.012$,
obtained with 20 expert-labelled training images and a different test set of
70 images.
