\chapter{\hybrid}
\label{ch:hybrid}
This chapter gives the results of \hybrid{}, described in
\cref{sec:hybrid_design}; \cref{ch:drivers} then tests which of its components
actually matter.

\section{Results on synthetic data}
On the synthetic data the model performs very well, but as
\cref{sec:gap} showed, this data is much easier than real observations, so
these results are mainly a check that the model trains correctly.
The version used in the rest of this report (8 filters, 593{,}842 parameters)
reached an \Fone{} of 0.9749.

\section{Results on LOFAR}
\label{sec:hybrid_lofar}

\subsection{Class weighting}
Without class weighting, the model reached a maximum \Fone{} of
$0.6603 \pm 0.0040$ over three seeds; with class weighting, it reached
$0.6467 \pm 0.0079$. So the model without class weighting is used from here on.

Interestingly, the area under the ROC curve moved the opposite way: it was
0.980 with class weighting and 0.938 without. The ROC curve measures how well the model ranks RFI pixels
above clean ones, while \Fone{} measures the quality of the final yes/no
decision. Class weighting improved the ranking but made the decisions worse,
which shows that a high ROC area alone does not mean a model flags RFI well.

\subsection{Comparison with published methods}
\Cref{tab:final} and \cref{fig:final} compare the final model with the other
methods on the 109 human-labelled test images. \hybrid{} reaches a maximum
\Fone{} of $0.6603 \pm 0.0040$; with the threshold fixed in advance on the
validation set, the pooled \Fone{} is $0.6561 \pm 0.0044$. This is 0.11 above
\tfunet{} and 0.062 above RFI-Net (0.5979), the best result reported by
Mesarcik et al.\ (one-sample $t = 27.2$ with 2 degrees of freedom). It is
achieved while training only on \aoflagger{}'s labels, which themselves score
0.5698 against the human expert.

These models were not all trained in the same way, however: the published
values use Mesarcik et al.'s training settings, while \tfunet{} and \hybrid{}
use our own. \Cref{sec:same_settings} therefore repeats the comparison with the
models trained under the authors' settings.

\subsection{Comparison under the authors' training settings}
\label{sec:same_settings}
To compare the models under the same conditions, we trained \hybrid{} with
the settings we used for Mesarcik et al.'s U-Net in
\cref{sec:mesarcik_rerun}: the 6621 training images without the test images,
their preprocessing, 32$\times$32 patches in batches of 1024, the Adam
optimiser with a constant learning rate of $10^{-3}$, 100 epochs, no class
weighting, the model after the last epoch, and the maximum \Fone{} on the 109
test images, with the predictions for the patches put back together into full
512$\times$512 images. Both models used the same preprocessed data and the same
order of training batches.

\tfunet{} cannot be trained on images as small as 32$\times$32. Instead we used
its closest run (\cref{sec:tfunet_lofar}) and took the model after the last
epoch, as the authors do. This gives a maximum \Fone{} of $0.5551 \pm 0.0155$.
All these results are in \cref{tab:final} (under ``Mesarcik et al.'s training
settings'') and \cref{fig:same_settings}.

Under these settings \hybrid{} reaches a maximum \Fone{} of 0.6258
(\cref{tab:final,fig:same_settings}), against 0.6166 for Mesarcik et
al.'s U-Net, with half as many parameters
(593{,}842 against 1{,}179{,}121). Trained with our own settings on
full images, the same network scores $0.6603 \pm 0.0040$ (\cref{tab:final}),
0.035 more than with the authors' settings in this single run.

With our own settings \hybrid{} also has the higher AUPRC (0.6583 against
0.5562), but \tfunet{} has the slightly higher AUROC (0.9470 against 0.9378; see
\cref{sec:dice}).

\section{Fine-tuning with expert labels}
\label{sec:expert_ft}
Every label used to train \hybrid{} comes from \aoflagger{}, which itself
scores only 0.5698 against the human expert, so part of the model's remaining
error may be learned from its labels. Expert labels exist only for the 109
test images. To measure what they add, the model must never be tested on an
image whose expert label it has learned from, so we used five-fold
cross-validation. We split the 109 images into five groups of about 22. In each
round, one group was set aside as the test images; the model was fine-tuned on
the expert labels of most of the other images (68--69), while 19 more were kept
aside to choose the best checkpoint. This was repeated five times, setting aside
a different group each time, so that every image was tested exactly once by a
model that had never seen its expert label. Images that are near copies of each
other were always put in the same group, so that a copy could not appear on
both sides.

\Fone{} over the 109 images from 0.6603 to $0.6747 \pm 0.0083$
(\cref{tab:expert_ft}), an improvement for all three seeds (by 0.012 to
0.020).
